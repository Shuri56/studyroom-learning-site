Unit learning competencies

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

  • compare Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry, and Lewis acid–base concepts and explain their limits;
  • identify acids, bases, and conjugate pairs in proton-transfer reactions;
  • write self-ionization equations and recognize amphiprotic species;
  • use Kw, pH, pOH, percent ionization, Ka, and Kb to describe aqueous acid–base equilibria;
  • explain the common-ion effect and buffer action, and calculate buffer pH;
  • predict whether salt solutions are acidic, basic, or neutral from their parent acid and base;
  • explain indicator colour changes, select indicators for titrations, and distinguish end point from equivalence point;
  • use equivalents, normality, and titration data to solve quantitative problems.

Key words in this unit

Proton (H+) — in these lessons, a proton is the positively charged hydrogen nucleus transferred from one particle to another during a Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction.

Aqueous solution (aq) — a solution in which water is the solvent. The label (aq) tells you that a substance is dissolved in water, where ions can move and participate in reactions.

Ionization — formation of ions when a molecular substance reacts with or dissolves in water; the extent may be nearly complete (strong) or partial (weak).

Equilibrium (⇌) — a dynamic state where forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, so concentrations remain steady.

Concentration — the amount of dissolved substance in a stated volume; in this unit, it is usually measured in moles per litre (mol L−1, or M).

1.1 Acid–Base Concepts

Competency

  • Define acids and bases using Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry, and Lewis models.
  • Identify conjugate acid–base pairs and amphiprotic species.
  • Write self-ionization equations for water and ammonia.
  • Choose the model that best explains a given reaction.

1.1.1 Arrhenius concept

  • An Arrhenius acid increases the concentration of hydrogen ions in water. In aqueous solution, the proton is associated with water as hydronium, H3O+.
  • An Arrhenius base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions, OH−, in water.
  • Limitation: this model is useful for aqueous solutions and neutralization reactions, but it does not describe reactions outside water or bases such as NH3 that contain no OH group.
HCl(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
NaOH(aq) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq)
  • Strong acids and bases ionize almost completely in water.
  • Weak acids and bases ionize only partly.
  • Strength describes the extent of ionization.
  • Concentration describes the amount of solute per volume. A concentrated solution can still contain a weak acid or base.
Limitation This definition describes substances in water. It does not account well for bases such as NH3, which has no OH− in its formula, or acid–base reactions that do not produce H3O+ and OH−.

1.1.2 Brønsted–Lowry concept

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton.
  • Acid–base reactions are proton-transfer reactions. This definition includes bases such as ammonia and goes beyond the rule that a base must produce OH−.
NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−
H2O donates H+ → acid; NH3 accepts H+ → base

Conjugate acid–base pairs

  • When an acid loses one proton, it becomes its conjugate base.
  • When a base gains one proton, it becomes its conjugate acid.
  • Members of a conjugate pair differ by exactly one H+. A stronger acid has a weaker conjugate base.
Worked example — label the pairs

For CH3COOH + H2O ⇌ CH3COO− + H3O+:

  1. CH3COOH donates H+, so it is the acid; CH3COO− is its conjugate base.
  2. H2O accepts H+, so it is the base; H3O+ is its conjugate acid.

Pairs: CH3COOH / CH3COO− and H3O+ / H2O.

Auto-ionization and amphiprotic species

  • Auto-ionization occurs when identical molecules transfer a proton between themselves.
  • Water is amphiprotic: it can donate or accept a proton.
  • An amphiprotic species can act as either a Brønsted–Lowry acid or base, depending on its reaction partner. Examples include HCO3−, HSO4−, and H2PO4−.
2H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)
2NH3(l) ⇌ NH4+ + NH2−

1.1.3 Lewis concept

  • A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair to form a bond.
  • A Lewis base donates an electron pair.
  • An electron pair is two outer-shell electrons shared together to make a new bond.
  • Unlike the Brønsted–Lowry model, the Lewis model does not require proton transfer.
BF3 + :NH3 → F3B←NH3
BF3 accepts the electron pair → Lewis acid
NH3 donates the electron pair → Lewis base
ModelAcidBaseUseful clue
ArrheniusRaises H3O+ in waterRaises OH− in waterAqueous ion formation
Brønsted–LowryProton donorProton acceptorTrack H+ transfer
LewisElectron-pair acceptorElectron-pair donorTrack a lone pair and bond formation
Limitation Brønsted–Lowry theory explains proton-transfer reactions, but it cannot describe every acid–base reaction. The Lewis model also includes electron-pair interactions such as BF3 accepting a pair from NH3, even though no proton is transferred.

Check your understanding · 1.1

Try these five questions after studying the acid–base concepts.

  1. According to the Arrhenius definition, what does an acid increase in water?
    Answer
    It increases the concentration of H3O+ (often written H+ in aqueous equations).
  2. Why does the Arrhenius definition not fully explain ammonia’s basic behavior?
    Answer
    NH3 contains no OH− in its formula. It acts as a base by accepting a proton from water, forming NH4+ and OH−.
  3. In NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−, identify the Brønsted–Lowry acid and base.
    Answer
    H2O donates H+, so it is the acid; NH3 accepts H+, so it is the base.
  4. What is the conjugate base of H2PO4−?
    Answer
    HPO42−. Removing one H+ leaves one fewer hydrogen and one additional negative charge.
  5. In BF3 + NH3 → F3B←NH3, which particle is the Lewis acid, and why?
    Answer
    BF3 is the Lewis acid because it accepts an electron pair from NH3 to form a bond.

1.2 Ionic Equilibria of Weak Acids and Bases

Competency

  • Use the water ion-product constant to relate hydronium and hydroxide concentrations.
  • Calculate pH, pOH, [H3O+], or [OH−].
  • Compare acid/base strength using ionization and equilibrium constants.
  • Set up equilibrium expressions and solve weak-acid or weak-base problems.

1.2.1 Ionization of water and Kw

  • Water ionizes slightly by proton transfer.
  • Liquid water is present in large excess, so its concentration is treated as constant in the equilibrium expression.
2H2O(l) ⇌ H3O+(aq) + OH−(aq)
Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C
  • Pure water at 25 °C: [H3O+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−7 M.
  • Acidic: [H3O+] > [OH−].
  • Basic: [OH−] > [H3O+].
  • Neutral: the two concentrations are equal. Kw depends on temperature.
Worked example — find hydroxide concentration

A solution at 25 °C has [H3O+] = 3.0 × 10−4 M. Find [OH−] and classify it.

  1. Rearrange Kw: [OH−] = Kw / [H3O+].
  2. [OH−] = (1.0 × 10−14) / (3.0 × 10−4) = 3.3 × 10−11 M.
  3. Hydronium concentration is greater, so the solution is acidic.

1.2.2 pH, pOH, and acid/base strength

pH = −log[H3O+]   |   pOH = −log[OH−]
[H3O+] = 10−pH   |   [OH−] = 10−pOH
At 25 °C: pH + pOH = 14.00
  • At 25 °C, pH < 7 is acidic, pH = 7 is neutral, and pH > 7 is basic.
  • These cutoffs shift with temperature because Kw changes.
  • A lower pH indicates a greater hydronium concentration.
  • Strength describes how much an acid or base ionizes; concentration describes how much solute is present in a given volume.
CategoryMeaningExamplesImportant distinction
Strong acid/baseIonizes or dissociates almost completely in waterHCl, HNO3; NaOH, KOHMost solute particles form ions
Weak acid/baseIonizes only partly and establishes equilibriumCH3COOH, HF; NH3Only some solute particles form ions
Concentrated/diluteDescribes amount of solute per volumeCan describe a strong or weak solutionConcentration is not the same as strength
Worked example — pH and pOH

A juice has [H3O+] = 5.0 × 10−3 M.

  1. pH = −log(5.0 × 10−3) = 2.30.
  2. pOH = 14.00 − 2.30 = 11.70.
  3. The solution is acidic because its pH is below 7 at 25 °C.

Percent ionization; Ka and Kb

For a weak acid HA: HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−. The acid-ionization constant measures the equilibrium extent of ionization. A larger Ka means a stronger weak acid. For a weak base B: B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−; a larger Kb means a stronger weak base.

Ka = [H3O+][A−] / [HA]
Kb = [BH+][OH−] / [B]
Percent ionization = (equilibrium amount ionized ÷ initial concentration) × 100%

Use an ICE table (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) to organize concentrations. In a weak-acid problem, if initial concentration is C and x ionizes, equilibrium concentrations are [HA] = C − x, [H3O+] = x, and [A−] = x. The same strategy applies to weak bases, solving for x = [OH−].

Worked example — weak acid approximation

Estimate the pH of 0.50 M HF at 25 °C if Ka = 6.8 × 10−4.

  1. Set up Ka = x²/(0.50 − x). If x is small relative to 0.50, approximate 0.50 − x ≈ 0.50.
  2. x ≈ √(Ka × 0.50) = √(3.4 × 10−4) = 1.84 × 10−2 M.
  3. pH ≈ −log(1.84 × 10−2) = 1.74.
  4. Check: percent ionization ≈ (0.0184/0.50) × 100 = 3.68%, below 5%; the approximation is acceptable.
Common error: Do not use the initial weak-acid concentration as [H3O+]. Find equilibrium x first. For strong acids, complete ionization is usually assumed; weak acids require equilibrium.

Use the equations and distinctions in this section.

  1. At 25 °C, [H3O+] = 2.0 × 10−5 M. Find [OH−].
    Answer
    [OH−] = Kw/[H3O+] = 1.0 × 10−14/(2.0 × 10−5) = 5.0 × 10−10 M.
  2. A solution has pH 3.00 at 25 °C. Find its pOH and classify it.
    Answer
    pOH = 14.00 − 3.00 = 11.00. It is acidic because pH < 7.
  3. Write the Ka expression for HA + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + A−.
    Answer
    Ka = [H3O+][A−]/[HA]. Liquid water is omitted because its concentration is effectively constant.
  4. At equal initial concentration, which produces more hydronium: a strong acid or a weak acid?
    Answer
    The strong acid, because it ionizes to a greater extent in water.
  5. A 0.20 M weak acid ionizes by 0.010 M. Calculate its percent ionization.
    Answer
    (0.010/0.20) × 100% = 5.0%.

1.3 Common-Ion Effect and Buffer Solutions

Competency

  • Explain how adding a common ion shifts an ionic equilibrium.
  • Describe buffer composition and buffer action.
  • Calculate buffer pH using the Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship.

1.3.1 Common-ion effect

  • The common-ion effect is the shift in an ionic equilibrium caused by adding a substance that supplies an ion already present in that equilibrium.
  • Apply Le Châtelier’s principle: the system shifts in the direction that reduces the added ion.
CH3COOH + H2O ⇌ CH3COO− + H3O+
  • Adding HCl increases [H3O+], a product, so the equilibrium shifts left and acetic-acid ionization decreases.
  • Adding sodium acetate increases [CH3COO−] and also shifts left.

1.3.2 Buffer solutions

  • A buffer resists a large pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added.
  • An acidic buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, for example CH3COOH / CH3COO−.
  • A basic buffer contains a weak base and its conjugate acid, for example NH3 / NH4+.
  • Added H+ is consumed by the conjugate base.
  • Added OH− is consumed by the weak acid.
  • The buffer has limited capacity; large additions can exhaust one component.
For a weak-acid buffer: pH = pKa + log([conjugate base]/[weak acid])
Worked example — acetic acid buffer

Find the pH of a buffer containing 0.20 M CH3COOH and 0.30 M CH3COO−. Use Ka = 1.8 × 10−5.

  1. pKa = −log(1.8 × 10−5) = 4.745.
  2. pH = 4.745 + log(0.30/0.20).
  3. pH = 4.745 + 0.176 = 4.92.

The buffer is acidic, as expected for a weak-acid/conjugate-base pair.

Calculation check: Use concentrations or mole amounts only when both species share the same final volume. Keep the ratio in the order base/acid for the pH form.

Check your understanding · 1.3

Answer these questions after the common-ion and buffer lesson.

  1. Define the common-ion effect.
    Answer
    It is the shift in an ionic equilibrium caused by adding a substance that supplies an ion already present in that equilibrium.
  2. When sodium acetate is added to acetic acid, which way does the equilibrium shift, and why?
    Answer
    It shifts left because CH3COO− is added as a product; the equilibrium shifts to reduce the added ion.
  3. What two components make an acidic buffer?
    Answer
    A weak acid and its conjugate base, such as CH3COOH and CH3COO−.
  4. In an acetic acid buffer, which component consumes added H+?
    Answer
    The conjugate base, CH3COO−, combines with H+ to form CH3COOH.
  5. A buffer has equal concentrations of weak acid and conjugate base. What is its pH relative to pKa?
    Answer
    pH = pKa, because log(1) = 0 in the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

1.4 Hydrolysis of Salts

Competency

  • Define salt hydrolysis and identify which salt ions react with water.
  • Predict acidic, basic, or neutral salt solutions from parent-acid and parent-base strengths.
  • Compare Ka and Kb for salts of weak acids and weak bases.
  • Salt hydrolysis is the reaction of a salt ion with water to produce an acidic or basic species.
  • Ions from strong acids and strong bases are generally too weak to react appreciably with water.
Parent acid/baseExample saltIon that hydrolysesExpected solution
Strong acid + strong baseNaClNeither ion appreciablyNeutral (about pH 7 at 25 °C)
Weak acid + strong baseCH3COONaCH3COO− accepts H+ from water, producing OH−Basic
Strong acid + weak baseNH4ClNH4+ donates H+ to water, producing H3O+Acidic
Weak acid + weak baseNH4HCOOBoth ionsCompare Ka and Kb: larger Ka acidic; larger Kb basic; equal approximately neutral
Worked reasoning — sodium acetate

Na+ comes from strong base NaOH and does not hydrolyse appreciably. Acetate is the conjugate base of weak acid CH3COOH:

CH3COO− + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + OH−

OH− forms, so the solution is basic.

Decision method: Split the salt into ions, identify each parent acid/base, then decide whether an ion is a significant conjugate acid or base. Do not assume every salt solution is neutral.

Check your understanding · 1.4

Classify salts by their parent acid/base and the ion that reacts with water.

  1. What is salt hydrolysis?
    Answer
    A reaction between one or both ions of a salt and water, which can change the solution’s acidity or basicity.
  2. Is NaCl solution acidic, basic, or approximately neutral at 25 °C?
    Answer
    Approximately neutral. Na+ and Cl− come from a strong base and strong acid and hydrolyse negligibly.
  3. Why is sodium acetate solution basic?
    Answer
    CH3COO− is the conjugate base of a weak acid. It reacts with water to produce OH−.
  4. Which ion makes NH4Cl solution acidic?
    Answer
    NH4+ reacts with water to produce H3O+; Cl− is a spectator ion.
  5. For a salt from a weak acid and weak base, how do you predict the solution’s nature?
    Answer
    Compare the relevant Ka of the cationic acid and Kb of the anionic base. Larger Ka gives acidic; larger Kb gives basic; similar values give near-neutral.

1.5 Acid–Base Indicators and Titrations

Competency

  • Explain indicator colour changes and select a suitable indicator range.
  • Calculate equivalents, normality, and acid/base quantities in neutralization.
  • Describe titration steps and distinguish the equivalence point from the end point.
  • Interpret the main features of acid–base titration curves.

1.5.1 Indicators

  • An acid–base indicator is usually a weak organic acid or base whose two forms have different colours.
  • Its equilibrium responds to pH, so the observed colour changes over a characteristic transition range.
HIn + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + In−
acid form HIn ⇌ base form In−
IndicatorAcid colourBase colourApproximate transition pH
Methyl orangeRedYellow3.2–4.4

PhenolphthaleinColourlessPink8.2–10.0
  • Select an indicator whose colour-change range lies within the steep pH change near the equivalence point of the titration.

1.5.2 Equivalents and normality

  • An equivalent is the reacting amount that supplies or reacts with one mole of H+ in the acid–base reaction being considered.
  • The n-factor is the number of H+ donated per acid formula unit or OH− supplied per base formula unit in that reaction.
  • Equivalent mass = molar mass ÷ n-factor.
  • Normality, N = equivalents of solute per litre of solution.
N = equivalents / volume (L)    and at neutralization: NacidVacid = NbaseVbase
  • Complete neutralization of H2SO4 involves two H+ per formula unit; NaOH involves one OH− per formula unit.
  • Always identify the reaction before assigning an equivalent factor.
Worked example — titration volume

What volume of 2.0 N NaOH neutralizes 25.0 mL of 2.70 N H2SO4?

  1. NaVa = NbVb.
  2. Vb = (2.70 × 25.0)/2.0 = 33.75 mL.
  3. To suitable significant figures, 33.8 mL NaOH is required.

1.5.3 Acid–base titration

  • A titration determines an unknown solution amount or concentration by reacting it with a standard solution of known concentration.
  • The standard solution delivered from a burette is the titrant; the measured sample in the flask is the analyte.
  • Equivalence point: stoichiometrically equivalent quantities of acid and base have reacted.
  • End point: the indicator changes colour. A suitable indicator makes this occur close to the equivalence point.
  • Titration curve: graph of solution pH against volume of titrant added. Its steep region marks the rapid pH change around equivalence.
Safe procedure summary
  1. Rinse the burette with titrant; fill it, remove the funnel, clear the tip bubble, and record the initial meniscus.
  2. Pipette a measured analyte volume into a flask and add a few drops of a suitable indicator.
  3. Add titrant while swirling. Near the end point, add it dropwise until a faint colour persists briefly.
  4. Record the final burette reading; volume delivered = final − initial reading. Repeat for consistent titres.
  5. Use the balanced equation and measured volumes to calculate the unknown concentration.

Safety: wear eye protection, use a pipette filler (never mouth-pipette), and rinse spills promptly according to laboratory instructions.

Check your understanding · 1.5

Review indicator choice, equivalents, and titration language.

  1. What does an acid–base indicator show?
    Answer
    It signals pH through a colour change between its differently coloured forms.
  2. What is the key rule for choosing an indicator for a titration?
    Answer
    Its transition pH range should lie within the steep pH-change region around the equivalence point.
  3. How does the equivalence point differ from the end point?
    Answer
    Equivalence is the stoichiometric condition where equivalent amounts have reacted. End point is the observed indicator colour change.
  4. What volume is delivered if a burette reading changes from 1.35 mL to 24.80 mL?
    Answer
    24.80 − 1.35 = 23.45 mL.
  5. What volume of 0.100 N NaOH neutralizes 20.0 mL of 0.150 N acid?
    Answer
    NaVa = NbVb; Vb = (0.150 × 20.0)/0.100 = 30.0 mL.

Unit Summary

  • Arrhenius: acids increase H3O+ in water; bases increase OH− in water. This model is limited to aqueous reactions and hydroxide-producing bases.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: acids donate a proton and bases accept a proton. Conjugate pairs differ by one H+; a stronger acid has a weaker conjugate base. Water is amphiprotic and can self-ionize.
  • Lewis: acids accept an electron pair and bases donate an electron pair. This model includes reactions with no proton transfer.
  • Water equilibrium: at 25 °C, Kw = [H3O+][OH−] = 1.0 × 10−14; pH + pOH = 14.00.
  • Weak acids and bases: ionize partially. Ka and Kb measure equilibrium strength; use an ICE setup and check any small-x approximation.
  • Common-ion effect and buffers: adding a common ion shifts equilibrium. A buffer contains a weak acid/base and its conjugate partner and consumes small additions of H+ or OH−.
  • Salt hydrolysis: strong acid + strong base gives an approximately neutral solution; weak acid + strong base gives a basic solution; strong acid + weak base gives an acidic solution. For weak/weak salts, compare Ka and Kb.
  • Indicators and titrations: choose an indicator whose colour-change interval is within the steep pH change near equivalence. The end point is the observed indicator change; the equivalence point is the stoichiometric condition.

Unit Review: exam-style practice

These original practice items are collected here at the end of the unit, following the sample’s unit-review approach.

1. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a species that:
A. releases OH− only   B. accepts H+   C. accepts an electron pair only   D. donates H+
Answer and reason
B. A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton.
2. Which pair is a conjugate acid–base pair?
A. H2O / OH−   B. H3O+ / OH−   C. HCl / NaCl   D. NH3 / OH−
Answer and reason
A. The pair differs by one proton: H2O loses H+ to become OH−.
3. At 25 °C, if [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−5 M, [H3O+] is:
A. 1.0 × 10−9 M   B. 1.0 × 10−5 M   C. 1.0 × 10−14 M   D. 1.0 × 109 M
Answer and working
Kw = [H3O+][OH−]. Therefore [H3O+] = 10−14/10−5 = 1.0 × 10−9 M. Answer A.
4. Which mixture is a buffer?
A. HCl and NaCl   B. NaOH and NaCl   C. CH3COOH and CH3COONa   D. HNO3 and KNO3
Answer and reason
C. It contains a weak acid and its conjugate base.
5. An aqueous NH4Cl solution is acidic because:
A. Cl− produces H3O+   B. NH4+ reacts with water to produce H3O+   C. Na+ hydrolyses   D. all salts are acidic
Answer and reason
B. NH4+ is the conjugate acid of weak base NH3 and donates a proton to water; Cl− is a spectator ion from a strong acid.
6. A titration indicator should be selected so that its transition interval:
A. is far below the equivalence-point pH   B. includes the steep pH-change region near equivalence   C. is always exactly pH 7   D. changes colour before any titrant is added
Answer and reason
B. The observed end point should be close to the stoichiometric equivalence point.
7. Calculation. A solution has pH 3.25 at 25 °C. Calculate [H3O+], pOH, and [OH−].
Show full working
[H3O+] = 10−3.25 = 5.62 × 10−4 M. pOH = 14.00 − 3.25 = 10.75. [OH−] = 10−10.75 = 1.78 × 10−11 M.
8. Short response. Explain why adding sodium acetate to acetic acid decreases the ionization of acetic acid.
Suggested answer
Sodium acetate dissociates and adds CH3COO−, a product in the acetic-acid ionization equilibrium. The common-ion effect shifts the equilibrium left, so less acetic acid ionizes.
9. Application. Classify NaCl, CH3COONa, and NH4Cl solutions as acidic, basic, or neutral. Explain the ion responsible in each salt that is not neutral.
Suggested answer
NaCl: approximately neutral; neither ion hydrolyses appreciably. CH3COONa: basic; acetate reacts with water to form OH−. NH4Cl: acidic; ammonium reacts with water to form H3O+.
